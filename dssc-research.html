Dye-Sensitized Solar Cell Production

Currently working on ChemE · Materials & Photovoltaics · 2026 – Present

Bio-inspired solar cell research focused on fabricating low-cost, environmentally friendly dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs) using titanium dioxide (TiO2) semiconductor pastes, natural anthocyanin fruit dyes, iodine-based electrolytes, and graphite counter electrodes. This work is organized into two phases: our initial group development at Rose-Hulman Institute of Technology and my ongoing continuation research.

01Initial Development — Operation Catapult 2026

Completed

Our initial research began as a four-person group project (Team 12 — "Berry Good Solar Cells") alongside Oliver Boghian, Devin Gross, and Walter Heeger during Operation Catapult 2026 at the Rose-Hulman Institute of Technology. Over the course of the summer engineering program, we iterated through six TiO2 paste formulations, compared rutile and anatase crystal structures on FTO and ITO conductive glass, and evaluated five berry-derived anthocyanin dyes for peak voltage and stability.

Final Paste Formulation & Assembly

Final Paste Composition (Trial 6)

  • 3.2 g TiO2 (Anatase)
  • 2.75 mL Ethanol
  • 1.0 mL Acetic acid
  • 5.0 mL Deionized water
  • 0.65 g Ethyl cellulose (binder)
  • 3.5 mL Ethylene glycol / PEG (slurry binder)
  • 0.2 mL Triton X-100 (dispersion & wetting agent)

Fabrication Workflow

  • Mix & Sonicate: Combine metal oxide, solvents, and binders, then sonicate to eliminate clumps.
  • Coat & Cure: Spread evenly on conductive glass (ITO/FTO), air dry to bind, then bake on a hotplate.
  • Sensitize: Soak the cured TiO2 slide in filtered berry juice to adsorb anthocyanin dye.
  • Assemble: Stack against a graphite-coated counter electrode with iodine electrolyte and clamp.

Final Research Paper · Team 12 (Operation Catapult 2026)

Introduction, Problem Statement, and Background Information

Solar power is the future of energy generation. However, solar panels need costly and finite materials like silicon, silver, and copper. We decided to create a solar cell using fruit dyes, titanium oxide pastes, an electrolyte solution, and graphite which are in abundance, cheaper, and more environmentally friendly. A dye-sensitive solar cell works when light passes through a glass layer to a dye. First, sunlight passes through a transparent glass layer to strike a natural fruit dye made of anthocyanins (the organic plant pigments found in berries). Anthocyanin is a good replacement for ruthenium-based dyes typically found in solar panels being currently used. Ruthenium is more effective but is a very harmful chemical if released into nature and water sources. While anthocyanin is not as effective, it is far more environmentally friendly and much easier to access, making it cheaper. The light energy hitting the top side of the panel (dyed with anthocyanin in our case) is converted to electrical energy with the help of the anthocyanin molecules in the paste. When the dye absorbs the light, it knocks electrons free. These loose electrons quickly jump into a semiconductor layer and travel through an external circuit to create an electric current. In our solar cell, our semiconductor layer was the titanium oxide paste. To keep the current flowing, a liquid electrolyte solution safely carries replacement electrons back to reset the anthocyanin dye layer. Finally, a thin layer of graphite acts as a catalyst on the opposite glass surface, speeding up the transfer of those electrons from the circuit back into the electrolyte. This complete loop allows a homemade or bio-inspired solar cell to continuously convert light into electricity using safe, plant-based materials.

Design Process

The first and most important step in making a solar cell is having a strong stable paste. In order to have an effective paste we tried many different combinations of chemicals with the goal of having the smoothest and most stable paste for our dye to soak into. Our biggest issues we found in making pastes were frequent cracks and weak paste structure. Our first three formulations lacked stability and wiped off easily, so we introduced ethyl cellulose and ethylene glycol as binding agents. Our approach was to first mix the measured amounts of the Glycol and cellulose to make first a thick gel that we then mixed into our main beaker with the ethanol, acetic acid, and TiO2. Our fourth attempt using this method worked well in the aspect of binding the material together and onto the glass, but the mixture was coarse and grainy making it hard to spread evenly and ineffective to dye. We concluded that the thickness of our glycol and cellulose binder was the problem as it was too thick making the mixture the texture we observed. In our fifth trial, we added more glycol to make the binder more of a slurry texture rather than a thick grainy goo like it was before. The result was a paste that we were able to spread evenly across the glass without clumps sticking out of the surface. While spreading, we did have a problem with some areas of the mixture containing more cellulose than others and therefore had some uneven binding when baked and complications during spreading. We added some Triton X-100 to the next trial in order to ensure the cellulose spreads evenly throughout the mixture making it more homogenous. The triton x-100 acted as a dispersion agent for the cellulose as well as a wetting agent not only making the mixture much more homogenous but also much easier to spread expediting the process of cell making. With a solid foundation and almost all of our complications resolved, we decided to switch the TiO2 to anatase from rutile. The reason why is that anatase has been observed to be more effective, and there was much more information and tests with it from previous years. We repeated trial 5 only replacing TiO2 (rutile) with TiO2 (anatase). We did run into some complications while baking as our first prototypes started to peel off of the glass when heat was applied and slightly turned a brown color. We figured out that we needed to first let the cells air dry so the binder has enough time to stick to the glass as applying heat too quickly doesn’t allow for this to happen before the paste dries. Also, the glass was a large factor as they used two types of glass, FTO and ITO. The FTO glass was smaller, yet worked better from what we observed. The ITO glass worked worse with more frequent defects in binding and more common cracks, but had a larger surface area. We used ITO because of its larger surface area since it did perform slightly better in terms of voltage. We produced 5 panels using the final paste on ITO glass that all hit a range of 0.65-0.78 volts, all using different berry juice dyes with different types of Anthocyanin in the dyes.

Results and Reflection

A solar cell with anatase TiO2 usually produced more volts than a solar cell with rutile TiO2. TiO2 anatase has a higher electron transport rate, which allows for more dye adsorption. There were three clear winners in the berries. They were strawberries, blackberries, and pomegranate. However, you can’t really define a winner due to the small amount and the variability in the tests. Strawberries produce the most voltage because they have a specific type of anthocyanin called pelargonidin-3-glucoside. This type of anthocyanin can bind tightly together on the surface. Also, strawberries are very acidic, which allows the TiO2 paste to be more positively charged. Additionally, strawberry juice is easy to extract, which allows for no physical barriers on the slide. This is the opposite of blackberries and blueberries which have a lot of pulp and skin that makes it harder to filter. This caused there to be “gunk” on the slide, which makes it less efficient. However, even with this “gunk”, blackberries still produced a high voltage because blackberry dye is purple-black. This allows for the dye to absorb a lot of the light. Additionally, it strongly absorbs light in the green-to-yellow range, which is a common range of light. Pomegranates performed with a high voltage too because the dye was really easy to extract. That means the dye was able to be applied really evenly. Additionally, pomegranates have delphinidin glycosides, which allow for a more reliable path for electrons to move. Furthermore, pomegranates and black-cherries have natural co-sensitizers. These co-sensitizers fill the blank spaces on the TiO2 paste where dye doesn’t stick. Moreover, co-sensitizers form a protective barrier that prevents the liquid electrolytes from directly touching the TiO2, which keeps current stable. There is one berry you shouldn’t use, and that’s blueberries. Not only is the paste really hard to extract, the blueberry dye produces a really low voltage. In the real-world, because strawberries and blackberries produce or have the opportunity to produce the most voltage, they work the best in places that need a high energy demand or where maintenance is easy. On the other hand, dye-sensitive solar cells are the best at being low-energy cheap solar cells. Therefore, these solar cells could be used in remote communities throughout the world to produce energy. Consequently, pomegranate dye would be the best type of dye because of its reliability and high voltage. The lesson our group learned is that through efficient trial and error and teamwork, you can constantly improve on concepts. If we had to do it again, our team would most likely use a capacitor in order to power an LED. Our current solar panels are too inconsistent, so allowing a capacitor to store up charge would allow for the LED to be powered for a quick second. No matter what dye or solar cell you use, it’s important to prioritize solar and renewable energy in the future.
